Import NHANES Data¶

We will explore NHANES 2015-2016, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, using two supplied tables:

  1. DEMO_I: demographics, including recorded sex, age, and household income.
  2. BMX_I: body measurements, including weight and lengths.

The data dictionaries tell us what the variables and numeric codes mean:

  • Demographic variables
  • Body measurements

Run this notebook from live_demos; the two .XPT files are in ../data. We will use simple unweighted sample summaries. NHANES population estimates require appropriate survey weights and attention to its sampling design; see CDC's weighting tutorial.

Do baby boys weigh more than baby girls on average?¶

The variables we need are:

Variable Meaning
RIDAGEMN Age in months at screening, released for young children
RIAGENDR Recorded male/female category: 1 = male, 2 = female
BMXWT Measured body weight, in kilograms

See the demographic codebook and weight documentation. We retain screening age for this example; the examination may happen later. For age at the weight measurement, RIDEXAGM would be the relevant age variable.

Comments¶

In these sampled babies, boys have a higher observed mean weight at every screening month from 0 through 12. Group sizes vary, so inspect the counts alongside the means; this does not mean every boy is heavier than every girl.

Each plotted point is a group mean, and the connecting line joins different babies at different ages. It is not an individual growth trajectory. errorbar=None keeps both plots focused on the descriptive means.

What is the relationship between height, leg length, and arm length? Does this relationship change with age?¶

RIDAGEYR records age in years at screening. The lengths are in centimeters: BMXHT is standing height, BMXLEG is upper leg length, and BMXARML is upper arm length.

  • Age documentation
  • Measurement documentation

These are not whole-leg and whole-arm measurements.

We choose adults aged 20 and older and require all three lengths so that each comparison uses the same records. Dropping rows is a decision about this question, rather than a rule for every NHANES analysis.

Height is on a different scale from the two limb lengths. To compare how the group means vary, subtract 128 cm from mean height before plotting.

This is an arbitrary vertical shift for display. It preserves height differences between groups; it does not make the three measurements equal or adjust for other variables.

Comments¶

Older age groups in this sample have lower mean height and upper-leg length; mean upper-arm length changes relatively little. The individual correlations also show that upper-arm length is strongly associated with height, even though its age-group means are fairly similar.

We are comparing different people, not following people as they age. These cross-sectional differences alone do not show how an individual's body changes over time; group composition and birth-cohort differences may also matter.

INDHHIN2 records household income brackets, not an individual's salary. Codes 77 and 99 mean refused and don't know. Codes 12 and 13 are valid but broad answers (20,000 dollars or more, and under 20,000 dollars) that overlap the detailed brackets. We exclude those four codes for this ordered-bracket display.

Map the retained codes to their dollar labels. Bracket widths differ, and the highest is open-ended, so equal plot spacing represents categories rather than equal dollar differences. Check the group counts alongside the means.

Comments¶

Mean height generally rises across the income brackets in this sample; mean weight has no similarly clear increasing pattern. These are unadjusted sample comparisons, so they do not isolate an effect of household income. For example, age and sex composition may differ across income groups.

A next question could be: does the height pattern persist when comparing similar age and sex groups? The plot helps us choose a follow-up question.
